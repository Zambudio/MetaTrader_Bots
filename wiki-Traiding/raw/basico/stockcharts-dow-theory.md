# Dow Theory (StockCharts ChartSchool)

## Overview
Dow Theory represents a framework developed by Charles Dow, refined by William Hamilton, and systematized by Robert Rhea. It "addresses not only technical analysis and price action, but also" market philosophy by examining how trends develop and behave.

## Three Key Assumptions

**Manipulation Cannot Affect Primary Trends**
"It was not possible to manipulate the primary trend," though short-term movements could be subject to market interference. The market operates too broadly for comprehensive manipulation.

**Markets Reflect All Available Information**
"Everything there is to know is already reflected in the markets through the price." Current valuations incorporate all participant expectations, earnings projections, and economic conditions.

**The Theory Is Not Infallible**
"Dow Theory is not a sure-fire means of beating the market." It functions as a set of guidelines requiring objective analysis and disciplined application.

## Three Market Movement Classifications

1. **Primary Movements**: Lasting months to years, representing the dominant trend direction (bull or bear markets)

2. **Secondary Movements**: Counter-trend reactions lasting weeks to months, typically retracing 33-67% of the primary move with sharper velocities

3. **Daily Fluctuations**: Movements spanning hours to days, possessing limited forecasting value individually

## Three Stages of Primary Bull Markets

- **Stage 1 (Accumulation)**: Pessimism persists; "smart money" quietly builds positions at depressed valuations
- **Stage 2 (Big Move)**: Longest phase with largest gains; "improving business conditions and increased valuations"
- **Stage 3 (Excess)**: Widespread public participation with "excessive speculation and inflationary pressures"

## Three Stages of Primary Bear Markets

- **Stage 1 (Distribution)**: Smart money exits while public remains; "business conditions are not quite as good"
- **Stage 2 (Big Move)**: Largest decline phase as conditions deteriorate
- **Stage 3 (Despair)**: All hope abandons; selling continues despite "low" valuations until capitulation

## Trend Identification: Peak and Trough Analysis

An uptrend requires "a series of rising peaks and rising troughs (higher highs and higher lows)." Conversely, downtrends feature "declining peaks and declining troughs (lower highs and lower lows)."

**Trend Confirmation Requirements:**
- Downtrend reversal: A higher low must form, then the previous reaction high must be surpassed
- Uptrend reversal: A lower low must form, then the previous low must be decisively broken

## Dual Average Confirmation Rule

"In order for a primary trend buy or sell signal to be valid, both the Industrial Average and the Rail Average must confirm each other." Historical context: railroads preceded industrial activity by requiring material transportation, making them leading indicators. Modern equivalents include transportation stocks reflecting "economic environment" sensitivity.

## Volume Analysis

- **Confirmation**: Volume should "increase in the direction of the primary trend"—heavier on advances in bull markets, heavier on declines in bear markets
- **Reversal Signals**: "High volume day after a long advance may signal that the trend is about to change"

## Trading Ranges ("Lines")

Horizontal consolidation zones represent "either accumulation or distribution," remaining neutral until directional breakouts clarify intent—upside breaks indicate accumulation; downside breaks indicate distribution.

## Historical Performance Context

A 70-year academic study found Dow Theory outperformed buy-and-hold by approximately 2% annually while carrying "significantly less risk," though recent 18-year performance lagged during extended bull markets.
